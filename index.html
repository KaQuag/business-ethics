01

Prudential vs. Moral

What do you do when doing the right thing might cost you something?

Two kinds of reasons

Prudentialwhat's good for mejob · salary · reputation
Moralwhat's good for othersthe "right" thing to do

Ethics is a daily choice. You choose it again and again, today too.

Is it wrong to think of yourself?

Not necessarily. Both kinds of reasons are real.

  • Caring for yourself is normal
  • Caring for others is moral
  • Knowing which one drives you is what matters

Nobody expects a superhero. What it costs you matters.

Me vs. everyone

What's best for me isn't always best for all. Me first, sometimes is fair when your livelihood is seriously at stake.

When the stakes are high

Approve a car part she knows might fail?

Refuseshe might lose her job
Approvepeople could get hurt

Integrity has a price.

Weighing it up

  • The more others could get hurt, the more it tips toward right.
  • Big risk to me, small harm to others → protect yourself.
Same scale, bigger stakes

What do you owe millions of people? Should global benefit beat profit? Closer to home: the VinFuture Prize (healthcare · energy · AI · environment).

Opening game recap: every A was prudential, every B was moral

  • 3. Two internship offers.Big firm, great pay, treats interns badly.Small NGO, low pay, a mission you love.

02

Evaluating reasons & the character audit

A survival instinct

We magnify threats to ourselves, which makes harming others feel easier to justify.

A business is a "legal person", not a person.

Ethics as a character audit

A reflective check of your own character.

  • Not just a personal choice
  • Not just a group belief
  • An obligation, for everyone

Seen clearly in the AI era

Fear of falling behind→Rush to adopt AI→Mass lay-offs→Costly rehiring

…all called modernisation.

Survival of the firm is not a free pass. Keeping a company alive never justifies harming innocent people.

Five questions for your audit

  1. Imagination Beyond the two obvious options?
  2. Balance Me vs. others, weighed fairly?
  3. Honesty Am I inflating my own risk?
  4. Autonomy Just following orders?
  5. Responsibility Am I part of the harm?

The final test

  • If everyone did this, would society be okay?
  • Could I say it out loud, without shame?
  • Imagine explaining it in court.
Discussion

If going beyond duty is optional, how is society protected? Can ethics be forced? Then why do we teach it?

03

Why ethics can't be regulated

Follow every law and you're automatically ethical? No.

Six reasons law isn't enough

  1. Law ≠ ethics Legal can still be harmful.
  2. Built-in limits Courts are a blunt tool.
  3. Slow & rigid Problems move faster.
  4. Lobbying Rules get watered down.
  5. Perverse incentives Secrecy, bare minimum.
  6. Moral ownership Set your own standard.

Minimum vs. standard

Compliance"What's the least we can do?"
Ethics"What's the right thing to do?"

The law sets the minimum. Ethics sets the standard.

04

The decision-making process

The ethical professional

You sit between employees, customers, community and business results.

Balance results with the well-being of people.

Case: the Tylenol crisis

Chicago area · 1982
31Mbottles recalled
7deaths from tampered capsules
  • Tampering happened after the factory.
  • Product came back with tamper-resistant packaging.
What J&J's choice teaches
  • Safety before profit
  • Accountability even when not at fault
  • Trust for the long run

Six steps to decide

  1. Name the issue
  2. List the facts
  3. Who's affected?
  4. What would they want?
  5. Test three options best case · worst case · who gets hurt · values given up · rules
  6. Decide

Slow down. Don't just go with your gut.

Worked example: the snack batch

A batch has the wrong expiry date and some may already be unsafe. Your manager says: "Just ship it. Nobody will notice."

1 · The issue

Sell something unsafe to save money?

2 · The facts

Wrong dates · costly recall · kids eat these

3 · Who's affected

Customers · company · manager · you

4 · What they want

Safe food · low cost · a trusted brand

5 · Test three options
OptionVerdictWhy
Ship it anywayruled outUnsafe and illegal.
Quietly bin itrisky for youDone behind your boss's back.
Escalate to QCbest choiceProtects customers, company and you.

6 · Decide: Option 3, the least harm and the most good. Weigh the scale properly. Don't let fear decide.

05

Moral obligation

Four things to remember

  1. Self-interest needn't hurt others.
  2. Laws can't guarantee good intentions.
  3. Living ethically is a choice.
  4. Our choices never affect just us.
The takeaway
Live in harmony, without harming others.

Ethics comes from the top. One day, the top will be you.

06

Secret ending: Who am I?

Every choice was a reflection of you. Can you see the gap between your thoughts and your actions?

Use yourself as the framework

No theories to memorise. Just practise it, every day.

Hard choices reveal us

Easy choiceOne option is clearly better.
Hard choiceNo option is better. Each is a different life.

Hardship shows your true nature, so be brave. Can't decide? That doesn't make you incompetent.

Choose from your identity

Not self-esteemshifts with your mood
But identityclear · consistent · stable

Based on "self-concept clarity", Campbell et al. (1996).

The self is bigger than you think

Your relationships, reputation, work and possessions are part of you too. Self-interest is only a small part of who you are. (William James, 1890)

What's scarier than a bad person?

An ignorant one. They think they're good, so they never get the chance to change.

After this course, ask yourself

  1. Do my actions match how I see myself?
  2. If not, what needs to change?
  3. Am I as ethical as I think I am?

No card matches that search.
