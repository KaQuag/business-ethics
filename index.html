Course intro

Why ethics in business matters

What is ethics, and how do we reason about right and wrong?

What is ethics?

There is no single, universal definition.

  • Spinoza: good is what helps us flourish. How can we live well?
  • W. D. Ross: right action comes from several prima facie duties: fidelity, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence. What ought I to do?
  • Wittgenstein: the enquiry into what is good, valuable and meaningful. What is worth living for?

Ethics is the systematic reflection on what is good, what is right, and how we ought to live and act.

Business ethics

"The study of business situations, activities, and decisions where the issues of right and wrong are addressed" (Crane et al., 2019).

From values to results
Integrity · Honesty · Fairness→Personal values→Business ethics→Trust · Sound decisions · Reputation

Lillie (2011): ethics is the normative science of human conduct in society, judging it right or wrong, good or bad.

Kohlberg's stages of moral development

The Heinz dilemma: should a husband steal a drug he can't afford to save his dying wife? What counts is the reasoning behind the yes or no.

LevelStageSounds like
Pre-conventional1 · Avoid punishment"I won't steal. I don't want to go to jail."
2 · What's in it for me?"I'll steal. My wife lives and I'm a hero."
Conventional3 · Roles and norms"A good husband would steal to save his wife."
4 · Follow rules, keep social order"I won't steal because it's against the law."
Post-conventional5 · Social contract"A law can't justify losing a human life."
6 · Universal ethical principles"Saving the most lives is always the best decision."

Scandals that made the case

  • Volkswagen cheated emissions tests.
  • Big banks manipulated interest-rate benchmarks used to price global financial products.
  • Commonwealth Bank's financial advice scandal.

Most surfaced after the 2008 financial crisis.

Questions to keep asking

  • Is following organisational norms more important than your personal principles?
  • Are organisational goals and personal morals mutually exclusive?
  • Can an ethical business be a profitable business?

Normative theories of ethics

Do we judge an action by its consequences, by duty, or by character?

Three families of theories

Virtue theories

Based on character

Aristotle's virtue
Develop virtues to become an "excellent" person.
Ethic of care
Caring for others, using traditionally "feminine" virtues such as care and selflessness.

Teleology

Based on consequences

Egoism
Achieve the moral agent's best long-term interest.
Utilitarianism
Achieve the greatest good for the greatest number.

Deontology

Based on duty

Kantian ethics
Universal law, good will, treat others as an end, never merely as a means.
W. D. Ross
When principles conflict, prioritise the most important.

Egoism

An action is right if, and only if, it best promotes the actor's self-interest.

Psychologicalshort-term self-interest
Ethicallong-term self-interest; may help others if that serves the agent
Apply it

Identify the moral agent (a person or a company), list positive and negative consequences for them, then weigh which serves their long-term interest.

Myths: egoists don't simply do what they like, and they can benefit others. Weakness: it looks only at the individual and can ignore blatant wrongs.

Utilitarianism

An action is moral only when it achieves the greatest good for the greatest number.

  • Counts unhappiness as well as happiness.
  • Judged on the cumulative total, not person by person.
  • What's acceptable can change with context.
  • Simple, flexible and democratic for organisations.
Critiques

How do we measure happiness? How far do we cast the net? It can demand otherwise immoral acts and harm a few for the many.

Kant's ethics

  • Good will: morality depends on intention, whatever the outcome.
  • Categorical imperative: act only on rules everyone could accept.
  • Humanity as an end, never merely a means.

At work: firm rules ("always tell the truth"), people at the centre of every decision, and acting from duty.

Critique: anyone working for a wage sells their labour. Are they being used as a means?

Ross, rights and reasoning

Ross: duties are prima facie and can conflict, so we must choose between them. For Kant, duties never conflict.

  • Legal rights come from a legal system.
  • Moral rights come from roles and relationships.
  • Human rights are universal, equal, inalienable and natural, such as life and free speech.

Good moral reasoning is logical, based on facts, and grounded in sound principles, not gut feeling.

Dilemma: the trolley and the bridge

Pulling a lever to divert a tram kills one to save five. Pushing a heavy man off a bridge does the same.

  • The moral question: if pulling the lever is right, why would pushing be wrong?
  • The psychological question: why do people pull the lever but not push the man?

Applying ethical theories

How do morals, values and virtues play out in real workplace cases?

The lifeboat task

A ship is sinking. One lifeboat holds six people, and eleven are left. As captain, who gets a place?

  • Morals: principles or habits about right and wrong conduct.
  • Values: a principle, standard or quality considered worthwhile.
  • Virtues: me vs. others.

"It's not hard to make decisions when you know what your values are." Roy Disney

Six workplace cases

  1. Employee absence Was the suspension fair? Is there ever a solution for working parents?
  2. Networking What do J.R.'s actions say about his ethics, and who should be concerned?
  3. Substance abuse Should Fred be reinstated, and is that fair to others?
  4. Workplace discrimination Should Marian quit, confront, report or sue?
  5. Purchase Can a VP skip bidding to buy from family?
  6. Social media at work Where is the line between personal expression and professional duty?

Law and morality

The law sometimes can't find who is guilty, for example when many people are involved. Ethics still asks the moral question.

Theories give our arguments a backbone, but our reasoning is also shaped by our values and upbringing: parents, teachers and the people around us.

Theory lenses for any case

  • Egoism: what serves the decision-maker best in the long run?
  • Utilitarianism: which option gives the most net good for everyone affected?
  • Kant: could the rule be universal? Is anyone used merely as a means?
  • Ross: which duty matters most here?
  • Virtue and care: what would an excellent, caring person do?

Three terms people mix up

  • Corporate governance: the rules, practices and processes by which a company is directed and controlled.
  • CSR: going beyond financial and economic obligations to cover social and environmental impact.
  • Corporate moral responsibility: whether a corporation can make moral decisions and be held responsible.

A corporation is a legal person: it owns property, makes profit, signs contracts and takes risks. But can it make moral decisions?

Narrow vs. broad view

NarrowMaximise profit, limit liability. Shareholder wealth comes first (Friedman).
BroadFirms are accountable for their social, political and economic impact, and depend on all stakeholders.
Stakeholders

Primary: employees, local community, suppliers, customers, shareholders. Secondary: government, legal bodies, the environment.

The three narrow-view arguments, and the pushback

ArgumentClaimCriticism
The invisible handAdam Smith and Friedman: firms chasing self-interest produce the greatest good overall.Public opinion, media and activists pressure firms to act responsibly, and new generations reward responsible brands.
Business can't handle itFirms lack the expertise and would impose materialistic values.Many firms do support community well-being, for example Qantas in medical research and food banks.
Let government do itGalbraith: the "visible hand" of government should bring firms to heel.Government is intrusive, lacks day-to-day knowledge, and its officials are not always virtuous.

Traditional CSR vs. Creating Shared Value

Traditional CSRCreating Shared Value
DriverDoing good, moral duty, reputationEconomic and social progress inside the core business

Global ethics & cross-cultural challenges

When a multinational works abroad, whose ethical standards should it follow?

Globalisation's ethical issues

Friedman (1999): the spread of free-market capitalism to almost every country, with a powerful backlash from those left behind.

  • Equality: WTO conditions can disadvantage weaker nations while powerful ones keep barriers.
  • Homogenised culture: should "Americanisation" be the price of joining the global market?
  • Distributive justice: developed nations consume most resources, mostly sourced from developing ones.

Multinationals (MNCs)

Many MNCs have more economic power than their host countries.

Host gainscapital, technology, expertise, skills
Host riskspower that can be abused

Moral issues: cultural differences, environmental damage, duties to local communities and employees, reparations, dumping unsafe products, and human rights such as fair wages, safety and unions.

Absolutism vs. relativism

Is an issue acceptable at home, and abroad? The absolutist applies the home standard everywhere; the relativist follows the host country.

Acceptable abroadNot acceptable abroad
Acceptable at homeAbsolutist ✓Relativist ✓Absolutist ✓Relativist ✗
Not acceptable at homeAbsolutist ✗Relativist ✓Absolutist ✗Relativist ✗

Integrative social contract theory

Donaldson & Dunfee (1994): define right conduct through a hypothetical social contract among members of economic communities.

Global ground rules + local community flexibility.

In practice: impact assessments, stakeholder engagement, social performance plans and reviews, local content plans, social investment plans.

Hofstede's cultural dimensions

  • Power distance: egalitarian ↔ embraces hierarchy
  • Individualism: collectivist ↔ individualist
  • Uncertainty avoidance: comfortable ↔ uncomfortable with uncertainty
  • Masculinity: nurture important ↔ power important
  • Long-term orientation: traditional, short-term ↔ futuristic, long-term

Class question: where does Vietnam sit, and what could it do better?

Gifts, bribes and what firms can do

Fadiman (HBR, 1986): in relationship-based cultures a gift can be etiquette. Bribes come as money, gifts, favours or entertainment, and firms can be drawn in without knowing.

  • Top management commits to the ethics program.
  • A written, explicit code of conduct.
  • An ethics committee gives the program an identity.
  • Formal training, with line managers as role models.

Business runs inside an ecosystem. Who pays to keep it working?

The damage

70%22%8%

1 billion people lack clean water; 2.4 billion have no sanitation.

Where business does harm

  • Agriculture: chemical pollution, soil erosion, lost seed and animal diversity, animal waste.
  • Water: intensive irrigation causes salination and pressure on water systems.
  • Air: asthma, lung cancer, heart disease, risk to pregnant women and children.
  • Climate: the hottest decade on record, melting ice, erratic weather, coral disease.

Why it happened

Business's traditional attitudes:

  • The environment treated as a "free and unlimited good".
  • Spillovers, or externalities.
  • Private interest over the common good.

Every organism affects its environment. Humans can upset the whole system.

Who should pay?

  • Those responsible? Those who benefit? Free riders vs. the social contract.
  • Cost–benefit analysis struggles: benefits are often aesthetic, uncertain and shaped by values.
  • We owe future generations a habitable world. But can people who don't exist yet have rights?

Four ways to reach environmental goals

MethodHow it worksDrawbacks
RegulationLegally enforceable standards.Hard to make equitable; relocation and shutdown costs can be high.
IncentivesTax breaks or matched grants; voluntary.Slow, and effectively a subsidy for polluters.
PricingCharge per unit of pollution, or tradable pollution permits. Pollution becomes a production cost.Puts the bill directly on polluters, who push back.
CombinationNo single fix is ideal, so mix methods.Must balance effectiveness with fairness to whoever pays.

In class: who pays for BHP's mine disaster in Brazil? And the poverty and pollution debate: "Valleys of Death", the Summers memo, and whether people who stay in polluted towns for jobs have really consented.

Ethics at work: moral issues & practices

What do employers and employees owe each other?

The employment relationship

Rooted in the old master–servant relationship. Implied duties: loyalty, obedience, diligence. The law of agency says act loyally, in good faith, and follow lawful instructions.

Morality requires neither blind loyalty nor total submission.

Abuse of position

  • Misusing company funds.
  • Insider trading: trading on non-public information. Unfair to outsiders and breaks fiduciary trust.
  • Trade secrets: company property. Theft is unfair competition; disclosure breaks confidentiality.

Conflict of interest

When personal interests are big enough to interfere with job duties. It is worrying even if the employee never acts on it.

Personal or family investment in suppliers, customers or distributors is a classic case; policies should deter it.

How to decide at work

  1. Identify your obligations, the ideals at stake and the effects of each option.
  2. Weigh where the emphasis should lie.
  3. Newspaper test Would you defend it if it were printed?
  4. Talk to a friend to check your bias.

Fairness and discrimination

Areas of concern: women in work, comparable worth, sexual harassment, unions, and discrimination by age, ethnicity, disability, genes, gender, class, religion, sexual orientation or politics.

Job discrimination can be intentional or unintentional, veiled or systemic. Parental leave, flexible work and childcare let parents compete fairly.

Case: Ellen Pao vs. Kleiner Perkins, gender bias in venture capital.

Privacy at work

  • Monitoring, email reading, vehicle tracking and social media checks threaten privacy.
  • Informed consent must be freely given and fully understood.
  • Personality tests, drug tests and wellness programs raise the same questions.
  • A firm's legitimate interest stops at what significantly affects work performance.

Personnel policies

  • Hiring: fair treatment, reliable and valid tests, no unfair discrimination.
  • Promotion: seniority vs. most qualified; nepotism.
  • Discipline and dismissal: just cause, due process, notice or severance.

Employees rank honest communication, recognition and respect above good pay.

Whistleblowing

Edward Snowden: traitor or hero?

Should whistleblowers be punished, protected or promoted? Are they good for their country?

Ethics, leadership & culture

How do leaders and culture make ethics stick inside an organisation?

Case: Vạn Thịnh Phát – SCB

5–8%of Vietnam's GDP lost
10 yrs2012–2022

Trương Mỹ Lan's group took hidden control of Saigon Commercial Bank and ran thousands of illegal loans through falsified documents and shell companies. Losses: 498,000–764,000 billion VND.

Deception, abuse of power, corruption, fraud and breach of fiduciary duty, while internal controls, auditors and regulators all failed.

Corporate culture (Schein)

  1. Artefacts The visible level: language, technology, clothing, manner of address, org chart.
  2. Espoused beliefs and values Strategies, goals and philosophies that guide behaviour.
  3. Underlying assumptions Taken-for-granted beliefs nobody debates.

Ignoring behaviour that breaks the norms can end up rewarding it.

Ethical culture

Five dimensions: role modelling by managers, capability (time and resources) to act ethically, commitment, openness to discuss issues, and reinforcement.

Hardwaredo's, don'ts and permissions
Softwarethe capacity and conditions that encourage ethics

Ethical leadership

"The demonstration of normatively appropriate conduct through personal actions and interpersonal relationships, and the promotion of such conduct to followers."

  • Personal morality: authenticity, integrity, courage.
  • Leader morality: ethics as a priority, and legitimate decisions.
  • Leader behaviour: openness, inclusiveness, visible role modelling.
  • Follower morality: ethical leaders need ethical followers, not groupthink or flattery.

Reputation is the most critical element. Leaders must be seen to be ethical.

Building ethics into the organisation (Shaw et al., 2013)

Ethical culture

Achieved through

Management commitment, capability, role modelling, openness, positive reinforcement.

Ethical leadership

Achieved through

Personal morality, leader morality, leader behaviour.

Institutionalised ethics

Achieved through

Codes, policies, training, reward systems, monitoring.

Perilous at the top

Leaders' overconfidence in their own morality can lead to downfall: Ken Lay, Jack Abramoff.

  • Apple "batterygate" (2017): updates throttled older iPhones.
  • Facebook–Cambridge Analytica (2018): data from 87 million users harvested without explicit consent.

A final thought

Financial performance is critical, but shareholders, employees, customers and communities all have to accept the way those profits are earned.

9.1

Prudential vs. Moral

What do you do when doing the right thing might cost you something?

Two kinds of reasons

Prudentialwhat's good for mejob · salary · reputation
Moralwhat's good for othersthe "right" thing to do

Ethics is a daily choice. You choose it again and again, today too.

Is it wrong to think of yourself?

Not necessarily. Both kinds of reasons are real.

  • Caring for yourself is normal
  • Caring for others is moral
  • Knowing which one drives you is what matters

Nobody expects a superhero. What it costs you matters.

Me vs. everyone

What's best for me isn't always best for all. Me first, sometimes is fair when your livelihood is seriously at stake.

When the stakes are high

Approve a car part she knows might fail?

Refuseshe might lose her job
Approvepeople could get hurt

Integrity has a price.

Weighing it up

  • The more others could get hurt, the more it tips toward right.
  • Big risk to me, small harm to others → protect yourself.
Same scale, bigger stakes

What do you owe millions of people? Should global benefit beat profit? Closer to home: the VinFuture Prize (healthcare · energy · AI · environment).

9.2

Evaluating reasons & the character audit

A survival instinct

We magnify threats to ourselves, which makes harming others feel easier to justify.

A business is a "legal person", not a person.

Ethics as a character audit

A reflective check of your own character.

  • Not just a personal choice
  • Not just a group belief
  • An obligation, for everyone

Seen clearly in the AI era

Fear of falling behind→Rush to adopt AI→Mass lay-offs→Costly rehiring

…all called modernisation.

Survival of the firm is not a free pass. Keeping a company alive never justifies harming innocent people.

Five questions for your audit

  1. Imagination Beyond the two obvious options?
  2. Balance Me vs. others, weighed fairly?
  3. Honesty Am I inflating my own risk?
  4. Autonomy Just following orders?
  5. Responsibility Am I part of the harm?

The final test

  • If everyone did this, would society be okay?
  • Could I say it out loud, without shame?
  • Imagine explaining it in court.
Discussion

If going beyond duty is optional, how is society protected? Can ethics be forced? Then why do we teach it?

9.3

Why ethics can't be regulated

Follow every law and you're automatically ethical? No.

The case

One app. One life. Zero laws broken?

A 14-year-old Character.AI user in Orlando, Florida, spent 10 months talking to a chatbot before his death in February 2024. Each reason below traces back to his case.

Six reasons law isn't enough

  1. Law ≠ ethics Legal can still be harmful.A chatbot romancing a 14-year-old dozens of times a day broke no rule in 2023.
  2. Built-in limits Courts are a blunt tool.The lawsuit after his death settled 15 months later: terms secret, no verdict.
  3. Slow & rigid Problems move faster.First chat April 2023; first state law January 2026; federal law still waiting.
  4. Lobbying Rules get watered down.Of two bills on AI companions for kids, the strict one was vetoed and the one that passed was weakened first.
  5. Perverse incentives Secrecy, bare minimum.About 2 hours a day per user pleased investors; teen safety changes came the day the lawsuit landed.
  6. Moral ownership Set your own standard.October 2025: Character.AI banned under-18 chats with no law requiring it. Why not at launch?

Minimum vs. standard

Compliance"What's the least we can do?"
Ethics"What's the right thing to do?"

The law sets the minimum. Ethics sets the standard.

9.4

The decision-making process

The ethical professional

You sit between employees, customers, community and business results.

Balance results with the well-being of people.

Case: the Tylenol crisis

Chicago area · 1982
31Mbottles recalled
7deaths from tampered capsules
  • Tampering happened after the factory.
  • Product came back with tamper-resistant packaging.
What J&J's choice teaches
  • Safety before profit
  • Accountability even when not at fault
  • Trust for the long run

Six steps to decide

  1. Name the issue
  2. List the facts
  3. Who's affected?
  4. What would they want?
  5. Test three options best case · worst case · who gets hurt · values given up · rules
  6. Decide

Slow down. Don't just go with your gut.

Worked example: the snack batch

A batch has the wrong expiry date and some may already be unsafe. Your manager says: "Just ship it. Nobody will notice."

1 · The issue

Sell something unsafe to save money?

2 · The facts

Wrong dates · costly recall · kids eat these

3 · Who's affected

Customers · company · manager · you

4 · What they want

Safe food · low cost · a trusted brand

5 · Test three options
OptionVerdictWhy
Ship it anywayruled outUnsafe and illegal.
Quietly bin itrisky for youDone behind your boss's back.
Escalate to QCbest choiceProtects customers, company and you.

6 · Decide: Option 3, the least harm and the most good. Weigh the scale properly. Don't let fear decide.

9.6

Secret ending: Who am I?

Every choice was a reflection of you. Can you see the gap between your thoughts and your actions?

Use yourself as the framework

No theories to memorise. Just practise it, every day.

Business is just a practice of expanding yourself.

Hard choices reveal us

Easy choiceOne option is clearly better.
Hard choiceNo option is better. Each is a different life.

Hardship shows your true nature, so be brave. Can't decide? That doesn't make you incompetent.

Choose from your identity

Not self-esteemshifts with your mood
But identityclear · consistent · stable

Based on "self-concept clarity", Campbell et al. (1996).

The self is bigger than you think

Your relationships, reputation, work and possessions are part of you too. Self-interest is only a small part of who you are. (William James, 1890)

What's scarier than a bad person?

An ignorant one. They think they're good, so they never get the chance to change.

After this course, ask yourself

  1. Do my actions match how I see myself?
  2. If not, what needs to change?
  3. Am I as ethical as I think I am?

The line-up game

A quick game to open the session. Very simple, just follow the order. Your choices shape the rest of today's lesson.

How it works

  1. Stand up at your table. Projector at the front, tables on both sides. Get ready to move.
  2. One question, two sides. Five questions, each with an A and a B answer.
  3. Join a line. Answer A? Join line A. Answer B? Join line B.
  4. No middle ground. Time's up? Freeze. Not in a line when time runs out? You join the shorter line.
  5. Last question: line up in order. First come, first in line. No cutting in.

Rules

  • Choose for yourself, not your friends.
  • No right or wrong answers.
  • Freeze when time's up.
  • Not in a line? Join the shorter one.
  • No cutting in.
  • Try to balance the two lines.

The five questions

  • 1. The café gives you 50,000₫ too much change, and you're already late for class.Keep walking. It's their mistake.Go back and return it.
  • 2. One group member did almost nothing. It's time for peer review.Full marks for everyone. Keep the peace.Rate honestly, even if it causes drama.
  • 3. You get two internship offers.Big firm, great pay, known for treating interns badly.Small NGO, lower pay, a mission you love.
  • 4. Your manager praises you in front of the CEO, for your teammate's idea.Say thanks and stay quiet.Correct it on the spot.
  • 5. Final question, line up in order: pick your motto."Take care of yourself first.""Do what's right for society first."
